The Big Story · The Fortnight the War Reached the Airports

RIYADH HIT, YANBU TARGETED, GULF CARGO CAPACITY DOWN 20%: THE WAR IS NOW AN AIRPORT STORY

Houthi missiles struck Riyadh's King Khalid International Airport around 8 October. The Loadstar reports three Saudi citizens killed, among them a Saudia captain, and a Saudia aircraft damaged on the ground; CBS puts the airport toll at 13 with at least 19 dead across Saudi Arabia in the following week (counts differ, treat as evolving). Najran and Jazan airports closed until 15 October, Abha went daytime only, Lufthansa and Air India suspended Riyadh, and Rotate data show Riyadh outbound freighter and widebody belly capacity down 34% in 24 hours (to 848 tonnes) with freighter only inbound capacity down 77%. On 16 October the Houthis attacked Riyadh, the Red Sea and Yanbu simultaneously (one ballistic missile, two cruise missiles and three drones intercepted), after a projectile struck Yanbu's North Terminal on 1 October, the first reported hit inside the port itself (attribution UNVERIFIED). Hormuz logged 23 attacks since 1 October, the busiest month since March, with transits at roughly 4 a day against a normal 85, and the IRGC claimed a supertanker hit a mine south of the strait on 20 October (UNVERIFIED). The market backdrop: WorldACD wk41 has Gulf markets capacity down 4% WoW and down 20% against the pre war baseline, Brent at $112.85 (+84% YoY) and jet fuel at $192.44 (LS ↗ · SBS ↗ · ACW ↗ · F ↗).

Why it matters: Riyadh is the kingdom's busiest cargo airport (576,600 tonnes in 2025) and its displaced tonnage must route somewhere, right as the peak builds. For a Dubai GSA this is the sales argument of the quarter: DWC transshipment, Gulf capacity you control, and war risk priced explicitly. It is also the cost story: every Saudi lane needs insurance language rechecked, and the surcharge cycle (Cathay +26%, JAL up 16 Oct) has only started catching up with $192 fuel.

War Risk · Diplomacy on Seven Conditions

Iran rejects US terms and sets seven conditions; Trump pledges no strikes before the midterms

Tehran rejected US backed Pakistani proposals until conditions including a region wide ceasefire, unfrozen assets and lifted oil sanctions are met; Araghchi says Hormuz reopens on "day six" of his plan if the US agrees. Trump publicly promised no strikes on Iran before the midterms, while CBS reports Pentagon planning for GBU-43 use if ordered. Breakthrough rumours were dismissed by Tehran as oil market manipulation.

Why it matters: a conditional path to reopening Hormuz exists on paper, but the strait logged its worst attack month since March. Price war risk as structural through Q4; treat any reopening headline as a rate event.
Money · The Hike Question Returns

CPI jumps to 3.7% on war energy costs; FOMC on 28 Oct prices ~16 to 18% hike odds, December 75 to 85%

September CPI (released 14 Oct): +0.6% MoM with energy +5.2% and gasoline +34.2% YoY; core 2.6% YoY. The Fed already hiked in September to 3.75 to 4.00%. The 10yr closed 8 Oct at 5.22% after touching ~5.35%; USD/JPY sits at 158.57 (20 Oct). Payrolls +29k confirmed the weak jobs side of the squeeze.

Why it matters: oil war inflation is now the Fed's problem, and a December hike into a weak labour market is stagflation pricing. Keep FX and interest clauses on every long dated quote; a hawkish 28 Oct surprise lands mid peak.
Trade · The Cliff Behind the Truce

The US China truce runs to 10 January, but China's rare earth suspension lapses 10 November

The post summit truce (tariff cuts on ~$30bn of goods, Section 301 port fees suspended, coal purchases pledged) holds past the midterms, but Beijing's suspension of its October 2025 rare earth export controls expires 10 November and does not renew automatically. Separately, Trump was sued on 20 October over the 50% Canada Section 338 tariffs (CNBC headline, details UNVERIFIED); the Canadian import bans stand since 29 September.

Why it matters: reinstated rare earth licensing would hit exactly the AI and semiconductor cargo carrying this peak. Watch 10 November like a rate event; the truce is narrower than it reads.
