War Risk · Claims Against Flows

The IRGC claims four tankers hit mines and are "still burning" in the strait while Bloomberg reports Hormuz flows near pre war levels: the gap between the claim tape and the flow data is now the whole war risk story

Wednesday's IRGC claim is the largest single maritime claim of the war: four oil tankers struck naval mines on the restricted southern route and were "still burning", two other ships halted, a drone downed (Gulf News live, 4 Nov, 07:48). No vessel names, no casualties, no independent confirmation: UNVERIFIED, like every Iranian maritime claim this cycle. Against it stands the quietest dataset of the war: Bloomberg reports physical flows through Hormuz near pre war levels and JPMorgan calls the market back in surplus (Business Standard, 5 Nov). The counts underneath remain messy and are carried openly: UKMTO 23 attacks since 1 October per the Global Energy Flow tracker (2 Nov) against CBS's "30 attacks in October, a record", an unreconciled conflict; the JMIC queue has no update beyond ~492 (25 Oct), explicit null; GEF also carries ~254 transits a week (about 70% below pre war) and an ~3% of hull value premium on the bypass route, the page is internally inconsistent, indicative only. The human ledger: Monday's dhow strike killed one Indian seafarer (CBS, 3 Nov), and a tanker took two projectiles off Oman's northeast coast the same day.

Why it matters: if flows are genuinely near pre war levels, the war risk premium is pricing claims, not losses, and it decays faster than anyone's model once a ceasefire prints. If the IRGC mine claim is even partly real, the premium is about to spike into falling crude. Nobody can hold both positions, which is why underwriters will lag: keep quoting war risk as sticky, and keep the fatality record and the UKMTO counts in every conversation where a customer waves the $93 handle.
