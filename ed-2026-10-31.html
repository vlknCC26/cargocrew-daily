War Risk · The Water

UKMTO rewrites Thursday's tanker hit: a small boat attached TWO limpet devices, the war's first successful limpet strike, and Friday brought two more burning vessels

Friday's UKMTO update materially changes Thursday's incident: not a projectile but a small boat that attached two explosive devices to the tanker's hull, causing an engine room fire that temporarily disabled the vessel, which later continued its journey (Iran International, 30 Oct; Arab News). Reuters sources call it the first known successful limpet mine strike in the waterway since the war began in February; one report says the tanker carried 27 Indian crew, all safe (single source, UNVERIFIED). Friday then added two incidents: a vessel struck on its port side ~40nm south of Lavan Island with a fire and all crew safe (UKMTO, via Gulf News), and the Royal Navy of Oman evacuating 25 crew from a burning Panama flagged vessel ~1.6nm off Musandam, cause unestablished. CENTCOM pushed back hard on the IRGC's minefield narrative: primary shipping routes are cleared of mines, 3,000+ commercial vessels have transited with US support over six months, and the IRGC claims of three (or four, Iranian counts conflict) mined tankers remain unverified by AP. Queue ~492 (JMIC, 25 Oct) is still the freshest count. War risk context: Hormuz premiums were already at 6 to 10% of hull value per voyage against 0.15 to 0.25% pre war (FT via brokers, early Oct), with cargo cover reaching 5 to 6% of cargo value; no insurer move specific to the limpet confirmation found yet.

Why it matters: a limpet attack from a small boat is attributable, repeatable, cheap and defeats the "cleared routes" assurance in a way free floating mines do not: it targets ships at anchor or slow transit, exactly where ~492 queued vessels sit. Expect war risk premia to test suspension levels on the next confirmed hit, which accelerates sea-air conversion demand and keeps DWC transshipment the hedge, while it also threatens the feeder flows that build it. Update the advisory language today: "limpet confirmed" reads very differently from "projectile".

Trade · Tomorrow's Fee, Next Week's Lapse

The EU's €2 per ITEM handling fee goes live tomorrow, stacking on July's €3 duty for up to €5 an item, and the China rare earth clock now reads 10 days with Beijing still silent

From 1 November the EU's "Union Handling Fee" charges €2 per distinct item (by customs classification, not per package) on non EU e-commerce consignments, on top of the temporary €3 customs duty in force since 1 July on sub €150 consignments, a combined up to €5 per item falling on declarants (sellers, platforms, importers), with bundling, minimum order values and a shift to EU warehousing the expected responses (Poland Insight, corroborated by the Council's own small parcels page; the per item mechanics are the detail most coverage misses). The rare earth clock: the 12 month suspension of China's October 2025 export controls ends 10 November, Beijing has said nothing about renewal, and the broader US China truce extension to 10 Jan 2027 did NOT name the rare earth suspension; January to September rare earth exports ran down 9.6% YoY and September magnet shipments to the US fell 28.7% MoM. On Section 338, the Court of International Trade challenge has a name and a theory: the Liberty Justice Center (which won last year's IEEPA case at the Supreme Court) filed 20 Oct for Gellert Global Group and a proposed importer class, arguing Section 301 supersedes 338, the products are unrelated to the alleged discrimination, and the 30 day waiting period was violated; it seeks an injunction and refunds on the 50% duties covering ~$20bn of Canadian goods. No post 30 Oct coverage of the revised list's first day was findable, an explicit null.

Why it matters: three trade clocks, all customer touches. The EU fee hits Chinese cross border e-commerce flows into Europe from tomorrow morning and the per item framing changes the math for multi SKU parcels; the rare earth lapse is the one that moves airfreight if licensing snaps back (magnets, EV components and defence adjacent electronics fly when export permissions wobble); and a serious CIT plaintiff with a Supreme Court win behind it means Canadian duty exposure could reverse mid peak, so flag the refund angle to affected shippers.
